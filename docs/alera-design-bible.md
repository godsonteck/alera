# Volume 0 — Reset & Constitution

## 1. Product Manifesto
ALERA is not a tool layered onto healthcare. It is a living operating system for care itself: a nervous system for decisions, coordination, memory, risk, and trust.

## 2. Design Manifesto
ALERA begins with the premise that healthcare software should not look like an administrative layer pasted over human work. It should feel like a calm intelligence that understands context, respects boundaries, and helps people act with clarity under pressure.

## 3. Ten Core Design Principles
1. Care is a system, not a screen.
2. Trust must be earned through clarity, not polish.
3. Every decision should reduce ambiguity.
4. The product must feel calm in crisis.
5. The interface must disappear into workflow.
6. Intelligence should feel collaborative, not authoritarian.
7. Complexity must be made navigable, not hidden.
8. Identity should emerge from purpose, not trend.
9. Software should extend human judgment, not replace it.
10. The strongest design is the one that feels inevitable.

## 4. Ten UX Principles

# Volume 1 — Product Philosophy & Reinvention

## 1. Product Truth
ALERA is the operating system of care continuity: a shared intelligence that turns fragmented healthcare work into one coherent, accountable, and human system of action.

## 2. Product Mission
ALERA exists to make healthcare more coherent, more accountable, and more humane by connecting the people, events, decisions, and risks that shape care into one trusted system of action.

## 3. Product Philosophy
ALERA is not built around software habits. It is built around the reality of care: uncertainty, urgency, continuity, responsibility, and human stakes.

## 4. User Groups
- Doctor
- Nurse
- Patient
- Hospital Administrator
- Laboratory Staff
- Pharmacy Staff
- Emergency Staff
- Operations Manager

## 5. Core Problems
- Fragmented information
- Slow coordination
- Unclear ownership
- Poor handoffs
- Delayed decisions
- Duplicate work
- Low trust in data
- Weak visibility across departments
- Disconnected communication
- Poor patient continuity
- Too much cognitive load

## 6. Desired Behaviour
ALERA should be proactive, structured, trustworthy, transparent, adaptive, precise, supportive, and predictable in core behaviour while flexible in workflow.

## 7. Emotional Target
ALERA should make users feel in control, informed, respected, reassured, efficient, confident, supported, and safe.

## 8. Product Positioning
ALERA creates a new category: the care continuity operating system.

## 9. Decision Rules
- If a feature does not improve clinical clarity, remove it.
- If an interaction slows a critical workflow, redesign it.
- If a product experience feels generic, it is not ready.
- If a workflow can be simplified without losing precision, simplify it.
- If AI creates confusion, reduce or remove it.
- If a user cannot understand the system quickly under pressure, it fails.

## 10. Non-Negotiable Principles
- Trust is the foundation of every interaction.
- Speed must never come at the cost of clarity.
- Clarity is more important than completeness.
- Safety is a product requirement, not a policy add-on.
- Responsibility must remain visible at all times.
- Continuity is a first-class product value.
- Simplicity must be earned through disciplined design.
- Intelligence must serve judgment, not replace it.
- Human dignity must never be compromised by efficiency.
- The system must remain usable under pressure.

## 11. Signature Idea
The Continuity Thread: a persistent structure connecting people, time, tasks, risk, and responsibility across the care journey.

## 12. What Success Looks Like
Success means product clarity, workflow efficiency, trust, adoption, clinical confidence, patient continuity, team coordination, perceived quality, memorability, and originality.

# Volume 2 — User Psychology

## 1. User Group Psychology
### Doctor
- Working mindset: diagnosis, probability, risk, timing, and consequence.
- Fears: missing a critical signal, acting on weak information, being surprised by change.
- Confidence: clear facts, visible ownership, and decisive next steps.

### Nurse
- Working mindset: continuity, monitoring, execution, handoffs, and practical follow-through.
- Fears: missing change, losing task track, unclear responsibility, delay during a shift.
- Confidence: clear status, reliable task ownership, visible updates.

### Patient
- Working mindset: fear, reassurance, understanding, next steps, dignity.
- Fears: being lost in the system, ignored, or overwhelmed by jargon.
- Confidence: plain language, visible progress, consistency.

### Hospital Administrator
- Working mindset: oversight, accountability, throughput, exceptions, and service continuity.
- Fears: silent failures, bottlenecks, poor compliance.
- Confidence: clear operational state and visible accountability.

### Laboratory Staff
- Working mindset: samples, status, accuracy, turnaround.
- Fears: mislabeling, delay, quality failure, unclear priority.
- Confidence: precise status, clear urgency, strong context.

### Pharmacy Staff
- Working mindset: validation, stock, dispensing, and safety.
- Fears: incorrect dispensing, unclear changes, incomplete context.
- Confidence: reliable medication history and safety review.

### Emergency Staff
- Working mindset: time, triage, priority, and escalation.
- Fears: missing key signals or losing coordination.
- Confidence: immediate clarity, strong prioritisation, trustworthy updates.

### Operations Manager
- Working mindset: flow, bottlenecks, coordination, and systemic improvement.
- Fears: hidden breakdowns, recurring exceptions, operational drift.
- Confidence: clear trends, useful exceptions, and change visibility.

### Finance / Billing Staff
- Working mindset: coverage, accuracy, coding, completion, and exception handling.
- Fears: missed information, coding errors, rework.
- Confidence: reliable status and complete history.

### AI Support Layer
- Working mindset: pattern recognition, context synthesis, risk indication, support without overreach.
- Fears: hallucination, false confidence, unsupported automation.
- Confidence: grounded reasoning, transparent provenance, explicit confidence.

## 2. Cognitive Load Rules
- Calm mode: show stable status, known next steps, and low-risk context.
- Routine mode: show current workflow state, pending tasks, recent changes, and ownership.
- High-pressure mode: show critical status, clear priorities, and the minimum necessary context.
- Emergency mode: show the smallest set of facts needed to act.
- Follow-up mode: show recent change, open needs, and recovery options.
- Review mode: show history, causality, and audit detail.

## 3. Trust Psychology
Trust is built by accuracy, speed, consistency, transparency, clear ownership, visible accountability, responsive systems, and graceful error handling.

## 4. Attention Psychology
Each role scans first for what matters to their immediate decision or action: risk, urgency, ownership, next step, recent change.

## 5. Decision Psychology
Users make decisions faster when context is visible, risk is clear, and the next action is obvious.

## 6. Mistake Psychology
Common mistakes come from missing change, unclear ownership, ambiguous states, weak hierarchy, and overloading the user with unfiltered information.

## 7. Urgency Psychology
Urgency should make users feel focused, not panicked. The system should simplify, sharpen, and direct action.

## 8. Patient Psychology
Patients need reassurance, understanding, simplicity, dignity, guidance, privacy, calm, and clarity of next steps.

## 9. Clinical Psychology
Healthcare professionals feel respected by software when it is fast without chaos, intelligent without arrogance, structured without rigidity, and supportive without interference.

## 10. Operational Psychology
Administrators and operations teams need visibility of exceptions, bottlenecks, accountability, and recovery from failure.

## 11. AI Psychology
AI should feel like a support layer, not a replacement for judgment.

## 12. Assisted vs Autonomous Behaviour
- Fully manual: critical decisions, approvals, consent, medication changes.
- Assisted: task prioritisation, handoff preparation, summarisation, follow-up drafting.
- Semi-automatic: routine updates, suggestion-based routing.
- Automatically suggested: likely next steps, risk alerts, missing-context prompts.
- Automatically completed only with approval: escalation, critical workflow transitions, medication-related changes.

## 13. Emotional Journey
Users should feel cautious at first, relieved on first success, supported during busy shifts, guided when something goes wrong, and reassured when results arrive.

## 14. Product Tone by Psychology
The product should speak in an authoritative, calm, concise, supportive, respectful, direct, and precise tone depending on the user’s context.

## 15. Behavioural Rules
1. Never interrupt a critical workflow with non-essential information.
2. Never make a user feel dumb for needing clarity.
3. Never hide the source of important information.
4. Never force a user to interpret unclear states.
5. Never use colour as the only meaning carrier.
6. Never increase urgency visually unless urgency truly exists.
7. Never overwhelm a patient with professional jargon.
8. Never present AI suggestions as final truth.
9. Never ask a user to repeat information that should already be known.
10. Never make ownership ambiguous when action is required.
11. Never make a user reconstruct context manually.
12. Never increase cognitive load for the sake of completeness.
13. Never create a false sense of certainty.
14. Never make a critical action feel casual.
15. Never hide an error once it has material impact.
16. Never make recovery harder than the original mistake.
17. Never create noise where clarity is needed.
18. Never treat routine work as dramatic.
19. Never treat urgent work as routine.
20. Never make a user guess what the system is trying to do.

## 16. Psychological Design Implications
Each role needs a system that preserves context, reduces ambiguity, supports the next action, and protects trust.

# Volume 4 — Information Architecture

## 1. Core Entities
The core entities include patient, organisation, department, care team, care episode, clinical encounter, diagnosis, observation, medication order, prescription, lab order, lab result, imaging, note, task, alert, message, care plan, referral, admission, discharge, transfer, inventory, billing, attachment, permission, audit event, and AI summary.

## 2. Entity Relationships
The system should make relationships between patient and clinician, patient and care team, encounter and diagnosis, order and result, task and owner, alert and severity, message and thread, and referral and destination explicit.

## 3. Hierarchy of Information
Top-level information should include patient identity and status, current care context, active risks, active tasks, current medications and pending orders, recent results, care team, and critical alerts.

## 4. Information Priority Rules
Urgency, severity, time sensitivity, unresolved state, ownership clarity, dependency blockers, abnormality, recent change, patient impact, and workflow-blocking issues should govern prioritisation.

## 5. Context Rules
A lab result without reference range or specimen context is incomplete. A medication order without prescriber, timing, route, and patient context is incomplete. A task without owner, due time, and purpose is incomplete.

## 6. Visibility Rules
Default to relevance, not completeness. Reveal supporting detail progressively. Hide low-value metadata until requested. Always show source where trust matters. Always show time where timing matters.

## 7. Grouping Rules
Group by patient, episode, encounter, department, task, urgency, owner, and source where each helps the current workflow.

## 8. Timeline Model
The timeline should show chronology, but it should be structured around meaningful events: clinical milestones, workflow milestones, communication events, state changes, alerts, scheduled events, and resolved events.

## 9. State Model
States include draft, pending, active, in progress, waiting, completed, signed off, reviewed, escalated, cancelled, overdue, critical, archived, error, and unknown.

## 10. Relationship Model
Relationships must be explicit and clear, not buried in data structure.

## 11. Search and Discovery Model
Users should search by patient, order, test result, task, note, alert, department, workflow, and status. Exact matches should rank above fuzzy ones.

## 12. Summarisation Model
Summaries should include what matters now, why it matters, who owns it, what changed, and what is next. AI-generated summaries should be labelled as such and traceable to evidence.

## 13. Prioritisation Model
Critical clinical risk, time-sensitive action, unresolved ownership, blocking dependency, recent change, patient impact, operations escalation, and routine follow-up should be ordered by importance and urgency.

## 14. Role-Based Information Needs
Each role needs different priority information: doctors need risk and current action; nurses need task and timing; patients need next steps and reassurance; administrators need exceptions and accountability; labs and pharmacies need clear priority and safety context.

## 15. Error and Uncertainty Model
Missing data, inconsistent data, delayed results, pending approvals, partial uploads, conflicting records, suspected errors, and low-confidence AI outputs should be clearly marked and explained.

## 16. Audit and Traceability Model
Every meaningful change should preserve who changed it, when, what changed, why, the previous value, and whether it was manual, automated, or AI-assisted.

## 17. Information Safety Model
Clinical decisions, medication changes, emergency alerts, identity data, legal or billing data, AI suggestions, and high-risk notes require extra care and traceability.

## 18. Information Principles
Every critical item must have an owner. Every action must show its consequence. Every result must show its source. Every record must have a clear state. Every summary must be traceable to source information.

# Volume 5 — Navigation System

## 1. Navigation Philosophy
Navigation exists to reduce thought, not create more of it. It is about reaching the next meaningful action with minimal friction.

## 2. Navigation Model
ALERA should use a hybrid model: direct navigation, contextual navigation, layered navigation, command navigation, and deep-link navigation.

## 3. Primary Navigation Rules
- Every critical workflow must have a direct path to completion.
- Every patient-centered workflow must begin from a patient anchor.
- Every high-risk action must have a clear route to confirmation or escalation.
- Navigation must preserve current patient or care context.
- Global search should be a route to action, not a generic retrieval tool.
- Deep links should preserve the context that the user would have had in the normal workflow.

## 4. Role-Based Navigation
Each role needs different entry points: doctors enter through active issues and active patient context; nurses through tasks and shift work; patients through current care state and next steps; administrators through exceptions and operational oversight.

## 5. Contextual Navigation
Navigation should adapt to patient, encounter, department, task, urgency, role, device, search, and AI context while remaining coherent.

## 6. Task-Driven Navigation
Tasks like review patient, prescribe medication, view lab result, sign off note, triage emergency, dispatch referral, approve request, and complete handoff each need a task-specific path and a clear action hierarchy.

## 7. Patient-Centric Navigation
The patient should be the primary anchor in care-focused navigation. Current care state, timeline, encounter, care team, documents, labs, medication, communication, and action should all remain connected to that anchor.

## 8. Search as Navigation
Search is navigation when it takes the user directly to a meaningful action or destination. Search results should preserve role-relevant context such as urgency, ownership, and patient state.

## 9. Command Navigation
Command navigation should support expert users and urgent workflows, but it should not become the only route to critical tasks.

## 10. Emergency Navigation
Emergency navigation should simplify aggressively, emphasise patient status, risk, escalation, and next action, and make rapid handoff and triage possible without distraction.

## 11. Mobile Navigation
Mobile should prioritise one-handed reach, quick status checks, short action sequences, rapid patient lookup, alerts, and urgent response.

## 12. Tablet Navigation
Tablet should support touch-first workflows, shared review, bedside review, and larger contextual surfaces.

## 13. Keyboard Navigation
Keyboard navigation should be predictable, deliberate, safe, and accessible. It should support fast jumps and expert movement.

## 14. Deep Link Navigation
Deep links should land the user in a meaningful state with preserved patient, task, alert, or workflow context.

## 15. Hierarchy of Destinations
Top-level destinations are patient, active work, urgent issues, current tasks, communications, and care continuity. Intermediate destinations include encounter, care episode, lab, medication, referral, handoff, and department workflow. Detail destinations include diagnosis, result, note, task detail, alert detail, document, and audit context.

## 16. Navigation Safety
Navigation must never make a destructive action feel routine, lose context, misroute the user, or make emergency transitions ambiguous.

## 17. Navigation Memory
The system should remember recent patients, tasks, departments, alerts, pinned workspaces, saved filters, and habitual workflows, while resetting unsafe or stale context.

## 18. Navigation Loss Rules
Never lose the user in deep hierarchy. Never force unnecessary backtracking. Never hide critical paths behind obscure menus. Never reset context without warning.

## 19. Navigation Principles
Navigation should respect context, shorten the path to action, adapt to urgency, preserve continuity, support both novice and expert users, and never feel like a dashboard template.

# Volume 6 — Interaction System

## 1. Interaction Philosophy
Interactions should reduce pressure, effort, and mistakes. They should feel clear, safe, predictable, efficient, respectful, low-friction, and purposeful.

## 2. Interaction Model
ALERA should use direct manipulation, progressive disclosure, layered interaction, context-aware interaction, command-driven interaction, batch interaction, guided interaction, assisted interaction, and review-based interaction depending on the task.

## 3. Principles of Response
The system should acknowledge actions quickly, make clear whether it received the action, show progress where needed, and explain failure and recovery clearly.

## 4. Feedback Model
Feedback should be defined for data entry, saving, submitting, assigning, escalating, approving, rejecting, sending, uploading, deleting, archiving, searching, filtering, AI suggestions, and emergency actions.

## 5. State Change Model
The system should show before, during, after, transitional, interrupted, failed, and recovered states clearly and calmly.

## 6. Control Types
Control types include buttons, toggles, selects, multi-selects, text inputs, date inputs, time inputs, range inputs, tabs, accordions, drawers, dialogs, menus, quick actions, chips, tags, timelines, tables, cards, forms, search fields, command palette, charts, file uploads, rich text notes, and notifications.

## 7. Confirmation Rules
Confirm destructive actions, irreversible actions, medication changes, patient identity changes, discharge actions, escalation, AI-assisted actions with material clinical impact, bulk actions, and cross-department actions that change ownership.

## 8. Undo and Recovery
Undo should support drafts, task movement, note edits, and low-risk updates. High-impact or concluded actions should require a clear recovery path rather than blind undo.

## 9. Progressive Disclosure
The system should reveal more detail only when the user needs it. Basic, extended, expert, emergency, review, and patient views should each expose the appropriate depth.

## 10. Batch vs Single Actions
Batch actions should be used when the risk is low and the workflow is repetitive. Single actions should be used when accountability, safety, or consequence is higher.

## 11. Async Interaction Model
Long-running actions such as background save, upload, sync, processing, summary generation, AI drafting, import/export, and report generation should maintain visibility and reduce uncertainty.

## 12. AI Interaction Model
AI should suggest, summarise, explain, ask for confirmation when material, stay silent when not needed, cite sources, mark uncertainty, and defer to human judgement in high-risk cases.

## 13. Alert Interaction Model
Alerts should be actionable, grouped when appropriate, dismissible when safe, and escalated with clear ownership and next action.

## 14. Collaboration Interaction Model
Collaboration should be traceable and purposeful through mentions, assignments, handoff, comments, notes, threads, shared review, sign-off, and status updates.

## 15. Form Interaction Model
Forms should feel calm and safe, with inline editing, autosave where appropriate, validation at the correct moment, explicit required fields, and clear permission states.

## 16. Table and List Interaction Model
Rows should be selectable, expandable, sortable, filterable, groupable, and searchable without becoming spreadsheet-like clutter.

## 17. Timeline Interaction Model
Timelines should show change, state progression, event relationships, and source context rather than just chronology.

## 18. Touch, Hover, and Keyboard Behaviour
The product should support mouse, touch, stylus, keyboard, and assistive technology without making one mode feel secondary.

## 19. Error Interaction Model
Errors should be clear, calm, recoverable, and explain what the user can do next.

## 20. Motion-to-Interaction Rules
Motion should explain state change, focus movement, expansion and collapse, urgency, and recovery without obscuring the underlying action.

## 21. Interaction Safety
Never let a critical action happen without clear context. Never hide consequences behind vague labels. Never let batch actions hide individual responsibility. Never make the wrong patient easy to act on.

## 22. Interaction Principles
Every action should make the next step obvious. Every state change should be visible. Every critical action should be reversible where possible. Every delay should be explained. Every shortcut should remain safe.

# Volume 7 — Workspace Philosophy

## 1. Workspace Philosophy
A workspace is the operational environment in which a person or team carries out a coherent unit of work. It exists to make one meaningful task feel possible in the midst of complexity.

## 2. Workspace Definition
Workspaces can be doctor, nurse, patient, laboratory, pharmacy, administrator, emergency, operations, billing, AI, review, handover, search, monitoring, or investigation workspaces. Some are persistent; some are ephemeral.

## 3. Workspace Model
ALERA should use a hybrid model of role-driven, task-driven, context-driven, adaptive, layered, ephemeral, and persistent workspaces.

## 4. Workspace Rules
Every workspace should have a clear purpose, preserve the object of work, make the current state obvious, expose the next action, hide non-essential detail, support collaboration, and preserve continuity across interruptions.

## 5. Role-Based Workspaces
Doctors need patient and risk focus; nurses need tasks and handoff focus; patients need simple care continuity; administrators need exceptions and flow; labs and pharmacies need safety and workflow focus; emergency staff need compressed and urgent focus.

## 6. Task-Driven Workspaces

## 7. Contextual Workspaces

## 8. Workspace States

## 9. Workspace Hierarchy
The most common workspace is the role-based operational workspace. The most powerful is the patient-centered care workspace. The most ephemeral is the emergency workspace.

## 10. Workspace Memory

## 11. Workspace Privacy

## 12. Workspace Collaboration

## 13. Workspace Intelligence
AI support should help with summaries, suggestions, task prioritisation, risk detection, anomaly surfacing, and next-step recommendation without taking over the workspace.

## 14. Workspace Modes
Routine care, review, emergency, triage, monitoring, editing, approval, handoff, patient explanation, and AI-assisted modes should each change the prominence of content, urgency, collaboration, and motion.

## 15. Workspace Composition
A workspace should have a main focus area, supporting context area, action area, history area, communication area, AI assistance area if needed, and global controls.

## 16. Workspace Continuity
Moving between workspaces should preserve patient identity, task state, urgency, audit trail, and collaboration context.

## 17. Workspace Safety
Never switch context without warning if work is in progress. Never lose unsaved critical data. Never confuse personal context with patient context. Never hide current state of work.

## 18. Workspace Principles
A workspace should reduce cognitive switching, preserve context, adapt to urgency, respect role differences, support collaboration, keep critical actions close to the work, and feel calm under pressure.

# Volume 13 — Iconography & Symbol Language

## 1. Iconography Philosophy
Icons exist to compress recurring meaning into a shape faster than a word can be read — never to fill visual space. Text is better whenever the concept is new, ambiguous, legally significant (consent, dosage, escalation), or used fewer than a handful of times per session; a symbol earns its place only after repetition has made it familiar. A symbol may replace a word once it has been learned through consistent, repeated pairing with that word elsewhere in the product — never on first appearance. Symbols must always support text in any action that is irreversible or safety-critical (discontinue medication, discharge, escalate) — icon alone is not sufficient permission to act. Icons create speed when they encode state at a glance (a filled dot vs. an outline dot for read/unread); they create confusion the moment two icons share a silhouette at small size, which is why silhouette testing at 16px is mandatory before any icon ships.

## 2. Icon Personality
ALERA icons should feel architectural and precise before they feel human or soft — the same restraint Volume 3 assigns to brand voice. Not surgical (too cold, too literal-medical), not organic (implies softness ALERA's clinical contexts don't want), but structured: consistent stroke geometry that reads as engineered rather than illustrated. A small amount of humanity is allowed only in patient-facing contexts (Section 11), never in clinician-facing ones, where speed and precision override warmth.

## 3. Icon Structure
Line icons, not filled, as the default — filled icons are reserved exclusively for active/selected navigation state and status dots, so "filled" itself carries meaning rather than being a stylistic choice made per-icon. Stroke weight is fixed at 1.5px at 24px base size, scaling proportionally (never a flat 1.5px at all sizes, which breaks at large sizes and disappears at small ones). Corners are consistently rounded at a small radius (1px at 24px grid) — sharp enough to read as precise, not so sharp it feels aggressive. All icons sit on a 24×24px grid with a 2px live-area margin, built for optical (not mathematical) balance — a circle and a square icon of "the same size" must look the same size, which requires the circle to overshoot the square's bounding box slightly. Internal spacing stays generous; ALERA icons should never feel cluttered at 16px, which is the real test size since that's where most of them will actually be seen (list rows, inline badges) — the 24px canvas is a drawing convenience, not the deployment size.

## 4. Icon Family System
Fourteen families, each with its own internal consistency but sharing the global stroke/grid rules above: Navigation, Healthcare Entities, Patient, Doctor, Laboratory, Pharmacy, Emergency, Communication, AI, Security, Data, Workflow, Administrative, Status, Action. A family exists when a group of icons will always appear near each other in the same context (e.g. all Lab icons appear inside lab-order and lab-result screens) — this lets each family share a distinguishing structural trait (Laboratory icons use a consistent vial/sample silhouette language; Pharmacy icons use a consistent capsule/bottle language) without those traits leaking into unrelated families.

## 5. Healthcare Symbol Language
Patient: a simple head-and-shoulders silhouette, never a full body, never gendered. Doctor/Nurse: distinguished from Patient only by a small structural marker (a collar/stethoscope-line abstraction for clinical roles) — never a literal stethoscope hanging on a neck, which reads as clip-art. Hospital/Clinic: an abstracted building mark with a cross integrated into the structure's negative space, not a bolted-on red cross (the free-standing red cross is the single most generic healthcare icon in existence and is banned outright). Laboratory: an abstracted vial silhouette. Medicine/Prescription: an abstracted capsule shape, distinct from the pharmacy family's bottle mark so "a medication order" and "the pharmacy department" never share a silhouette. Diagnosis: a magnifier-adjacent mark, but built from ALERA's own grid geometry rather than a stock search-icon reskin. Vitals: a single clean waveform line, not a heart shape (hearts are the second most overused cliché after the red cross). Emergency/Ambulance: covered in Section 10. Appointment: a calendar mark with a single accent dot, never a full illustrated calendar page. Referral: two nodes connected by a directional line — this reuses the Care Network Graph's own visual grammar (Section 6) rather than inventing a separate metaphor. Care team: three overlapping circles, abstracted, not literal person icons stacked. Medical record/Timeline: a horizontal line with discrete markers, again borrowed from the same structural language as the Care Network Graph and the Volume 4 timeline model, so "this is a sequence of meaningful events" looks the same everywhere it appears.

## 6. Care Network Graph Symbols
This is ALERA's signature visual element and should be treated as such — recognisable even out of context, the way a specific chart type becomes associated with a specific product. Nodes: patient nodes are circular; institutional nodes (hospital, lab, pharmacy) are rounded-square, so a user can tell "person" from "place" by silhouette alone before reading a label. Ambulance nodes carry the Section 10 emergency marker inline. Relationship lines: solid line = active/confirmed relationship; dashed line = AI-detected or unconfirmed relationship (directly reusing the "dashed = suggested, solid = confirmed" rule from Volume 14 §6); line weight scales with relationship strength/frequency, never with arbitrary decoration. Trust indicators live on the node itself as a small ring treatment (solid ring = verified, dotted ring = unverified) rather than as separate icons, keeping the graph's visual density low. States: active nodes at full opacity and full colour; inactive nodes desaturated to the neutral surface tone; urgent nodes get the `state-critical` ring and a subtle pulse (Section 13) — colour and shape both change together, never colour alone, per Volume 7's accessibility principle.

## 7. Action Icons
Each verb gets exactly one icon, used only for that verb, everywhere in the product — Create, Edit, Review, Approve, Reject, Assign, Share, Send, Escalate, Archive, Delete, Download, Upload, Compare, Analyse, Explain, Confirm, Cancel. Reversible actions (Edit, Archive, Cancel) use neutral line icons. Irreversible or consequential actions (Delete, Reject, Escalate) always pair the icon with a text label in the interface itself, never icon-only — the icon alone is a wayfinding aid, not sufficient confirmation for something that can't be undone. Approve/Confirm use a consistent check-mark geometry; Reject/Cancel use a consistent X geometry at the same visual weight, so neither reads as more or less severe than it actually is (an oversized red X next to an undersized grey check would bias the user, which Volume 2's behavioural rules explicitly forbid).

## 8. Status Symbols
Every state in Volume 4 §9 gets a dedicated shape-plus-colour pairing, never colour alone: Success — filled circle + check. Warning — filled triangle + line. Critical — filled triangle + double-line (visually distinct from Warning's triangle at a glance, not just a colour swap). Pending — outline circle, empty. Completed — filled circle + check (same mark as Success, since completed work is a success state). Blocked — filled circle + horizontal bar. Draft — dashed outline circle. Verified — filled circle + check + small ring (distinguishes "verified" from plain "success"). Review needed — outline circle + question mark. Expired — outline circle + diagonal line through it. Offline — outline circle + disconnected-line mark. Syncing — outline circle with a partial arc, animated per Section 13. AI generated — dashed border treatment applied to whatever container holds the content, not a standalone icon (consistent with Volume 14 §6). Human reviewed — the AI dashed border resolves to solid once reviewed, which is a state transition users can watch happen, not just read about.

## 9. AI Symbol Language
AI presence is always marked by the same small consistent glyph (a simple abstracted spark/node form, never a literal robot, brain, or magic-wand icon — those three are explicitly rejected as overclaiming what the system actually is). AI suggestions use the dashed-border convention already established. AI confidence is never shown as a numeric percentage badge sitting on an icon (false precision); instead confidence is shown as a three-step fill state on the AI glyph itself — low, medium, high — communicating "how sure" without pretending to a precision the model doesn't have. AI-generated content is always labelled at the container level, not buried in an icon a user might miss. AI limitations surface as a small "i" info mark next to the AI glyph, opening an explanation on demand — the system should always be one tap from explaining itself. AI approval states reuse the dashed/solid convention from Section 8.

## 10. Emergency Symbol Language
Emergency symbols must attract attention through shape and weight, not through animation intensity or saturation alone — a static, well-weighted triangle mark communicates seriousness better than a flashing icon, which reads as panic rather than urgency (directly enforcing Volume 12 §12's "increase attention without panic"). The emergency mark is a filled triangle with a bold vertical bar, used consistently for the Emergency family, Critical status, and Ambulance nodes so a user under pressure only has to recognise one shape. Avoid: flashing/strobing treatments, red-on-red combinations that vibrate optically, and multiple different "urgent" shapes competing for the same meaning.

## 11. Patient-Facing Icon Rules
Patient-facing icons may soften the architectural rigidity slightly — marginally thicker strokes, marginally more generous rounding — so the same underlying grid feels a little warmer without becoming a different system. No technical/clinical iconography (no vial, no waveform, no clinical status shapes) should appear in patient-facing surfaces unless directly explained in plain language alongside it. Culturally, symbols should avoid any gesture, hand shape, or figure that carries different meaning across the user base ALERA actually serves — when in doubt, prefer abstract geometric marks over any figurative human depiction.

## 12. Clinician-Facing Icon Rules
Clinician-facing icons stay at full structural rigidity — fast to recognise at small size, information-dense where the workflow demands it (e.g. a lab-result row may legitimately carry three status marks at once: specimen state, result state, review state). Speed of recognition outranks aesthetic breathing room in these contexts; this is the one place where icon density is allowed to be higher than Volume 0's general "reduce cognitive load" principle would otherwise suggest, because the alternative — clinicians re-reading text under time pressure — is worse.

## 13. Icon Animation Rules
Only four icon animations exist in the whole system, each mapped to a Volume 14 motion tier: active-state fill transitions use Fast (120ms); loading uses the Syncing icon's partial arc rotating continuously at a slow, even rate (background tier, never fast-spinning); success confirmation plays a single one-shot check-draw at Normal (200ms) and never loops; alert/critical icons get a single restrained pulse (opacity 100%→85%→100%) at a slow rate, capped at three repetitions before settling to static — an icon that pulses forever trains users to ignore it, which defeats its purpose.

## 14. Icon Colour Rules
Default icon colour is neutral (`text-medium` from Volume 14) in every context except: active/selected navigation state (brand-secondary), semantic status icons (their assigned semantic token), and the AI glyph (`state-ai`). Icons are never coloured for decoration or to "brighten up" a screen — an icon's colour is always meaningful, and if it isn't, it should be neutral. Disabled icons use `text-disabled` and are never simply the active colour at reduced opacity, since that fails colour-blind and low-vision users identically to how Volume 14 §6 already rules out for buttons.

## 15. Accessibility
Every icon used without adjacent text must carry a state or shape difference redundant with colour — this is non-negotiable and is checked explicitly in Section 18. Icons scale via the same type-scale multipliers as text, never independently, so a user who increases text size gets proportionally larger icons too. Touch targets are a minimum 44×44pt regardless of the visual icon being 16–24px inside that target. Every icon exposes a screen-reader label describing its meaning ("Critical alert," not "Triangle icon"). Tooltips appear on hover/long-press for any icon-only control, using the same label as the screen-reader text so sighted and non-sighted users get identical information.

## 16. Anti-Generic Rules
1. Never use a stock icon library unmodified.
2. Never mix line and filled styles outside the fill-means-active convention.
3. Never use icons where a first-time user would need text to understand them.
4. Never use a red cross as a healthcare mark.
5. Never use a heart shape for vitals or health generally.
6. Never use a literal stethoscope, thermometer, or syringe as clip-art.
7. Never use a robot, brain, or magic wand to represent AI.
8. Never use a lightbulb for "idea" or "insight" — it's the most overused metaphor in software.
9. Never use a shield alone for "security" without a supporting lock or check mark.
10. Never use a rocket ship for "launch" or "growth."
11. Never use a gear alone for "settings" without distinguishing it from "process" or "automation" contexts.
12. Never use emoji or emoji-style icons anywhere in the product.
13. Never let two icons in active use share the same silhouette at 16px.
14. Never introduce a new icon for a concept that already has one.
15. Never use a different stroke weight for "just this one icon."
16. Never use drop shadows or gradients inside an icon itself.
17. Never use three-dimensional or skeuomorphic icon treatments.
18. Never animate an icon purely for delight with no state meaning behind it.
19. Never use colour on an icon that isn't semantically assigned.
20. Never use a smiling or frowning face for status — status is not an emotion.
21. Never use a check mark for anything except confirmed/approved/success states.
22. Never use an X mark for anything except reject/cancel/error states.
23. Never let a family's internal metaphor (e.g. Pharmacy's bottle) bleed into another family.
24. Never reuse the emergency triangle-and-bar mark for non-emergency warnings.
25. Never use a padlock icon for anything other than literal access/security.
26. Never use a magnifying glass for anything except search or literal inspection.
27. Never use an eye icon for both "view" and "visibility/privacy" — pick one meaning system-wide.
28. Never use arrows with inconsistent direction conventions (left always means back/previous).
29. Never let patient-facing icons carry clinical density.
30. Never let clinician-facing icons be softened for aesthetics at the cost of recognition speed.
31. Never ship an icon that hasn't been tested at 16px.
32. Never ship an icon that hasn't been tested in dark mode.
33. Never use culturally specific gestures or hand shapes.
34. Never use a country flag as a language or region selector without a text label.
35. Never let an icon imply certainty the underlying data doesn't have.
36. Never use a flashing or strobing animation for urgency.
37. Never let an AI icon look more confident than the AI's actual confidence state.
38. Never use identical icons for two different severities (Warning vs Critical must be visually distinct, not just recolored).
39. Never introduce an icon-only irreversible action with no text confirmation.
40. Never let the icon set grow without a documented addition — every new icon needs a stated reason it can't be served by an existing one.

## 17. Icon Consistency Rules
Consistent across the entire system, without exception: 24px grid and 1.5px stroke ratio; corner radius treatment; the fill-means-active convention; the dashed-means-suggested/solid-means-confirmed convention; naming (every icon has one canonical name used identically in code, design files, and documentation — never a synonym drift like "warn" in one place and "caution" in another); accessibility label wording; and the rule that meaning is never carried by colour alone.

## 18. Icon QA Rules
Before any icon ships, check: recognition speed (can a new user identify it in under one second without a label, for icons used icon-only); ambiguity (does it get confused with any other icon in the set at 16px, in both light and dark mode); consistency (does it obey Section 17 without exception); accessibility (does it pass the redundant-encoding test in Section 15); healthcare appropriateness (does it avoid every item in Section 16's banned list); uniqueness (would this icon look at home in a generic project-management SaaS product — if yes, it needs more ALERA-specific structure); scalability (does it hold up from 12px to 48px without becoming a blob or losing its identifying feature); emotional tone (does a patient-facing version of this icon feel calm, and does a clinician-facing version feel fast and precise, per Sections 11–12).

---

**Self-review, applied honestly:** This system is recognisable without text mainly through the Care Network Graph node/line grammar and the dashed-vs-solid AI convention — those two ideas repeat often enough to become identity. It avoids generic SaaS icon sets by banning the specific clichés (red cross, heart, robot, lightbulb) that make most healthcare and AI products look interchangeable. A healthcare professional would understand the Status and Action families quickly since they map directly onto states already familiar from Volume 4. Safety is supported by the irreversible-action pairing rule and the redundant colour-plus-shape rule. It reads as a complete language rather than a component afterthought — the one open risk is that no actual icon has been drawn yet; everything above is the specification an illustrator or icon designer would build from, not the icons themselves.

## 5. Motion Tokens

Directly implements Volume 12 §14's named tiers with real numbers:

| Tier | Duration | Easing | Use |

No display typeface has been chosen — Volume 3's brand personality needs a decision here (custom vs. a licensed serious sans like Söhne, Inter, or IBM Plex Sans) before final polish; system-font default above is a placeholder, not the final call. # Volume 15 — Component Library Specification

## 1. Component Philosophy
A component exists once a pattern repeats three or more times across the product — before that, it's a one-off layout, not a component. Every component below is specified against Volume 14 tokens directly; no component should introduce a colour, spacing, or radius value that isn't already a token.

## 2. Buttons
Primary (brand-primary fill, white text, `radius-sm`), Secondary (1px `brand-primary` border, transparent fill), Tertiary/text-only (no border, `text-interactive` colour), Destructive (`state-danger` fill, reserved for actions that also carry a text confirmation per Volume 13 §7). States: default, hover (darken per Volume 14 §1 hover tokens), focus-visible (2px outline), active/pressed (darken further, 1px inset shift), disabled (`text-disabled` + `surface-secondary`, never opacity-only), loading (spinner replaces label, button width locked to prevent layout shift). Minimum touch target 44×44pt regardless of visual size. Icon-only buttons always carry a tooltip and screen-reader label.

## 3. Inputs
Text input, select, textarea, date picker, search: 1px border in `text-low` default, `brand-secondary` on focus with 2px outline, `state-danger` border + inline message on error (never colour-only — an error icon and text always accompany the red border). Placeholder text uses `text-low`, never `text-disabled` (placeholders are not disabled state). Labels sit above the field, always visible — never inside the field as a vanishing placeholder, since that fails the "no critical task on one channel" test the moment the user starts typing and forgets what the field was.

## 4. Cards & Panels
`surface-elevated`, `radius-md`, `elev-1` default, `elev-2` on hover only where the card is itself interactive (clickable card, not a static info card). Padding `space-4`. A card's header always states what it is and, where relevant, its state (draft/pending/critical) via the Volume 13 status-symbol convention, never colour alone.

## 5. Tables
Used for any list where users compare rows across shared columns (lab results, medication lists, task queues). Row height fixed at 44px minimum for touch parity. `type-data` for numeric/ID columns with tabular figures. Zebra striping is avoided (Volume 8 already treats it as decorative noise) in favour of a 1px hairline row divider in `surface-secondary`. Sortable columns show a static sort-direction icon, not an ambiguous double-arrow that doesn't indicate current state.

## 6. Modals & Dialogs
`surface-modal`, `radius-lg`, `elev-3`, entrance at Deliberate motion tier (280ms). Confirmation dialogs for irreversible actions always restate the action in plain text ("This will discontinue the patient's current prescription") — never a generic "Are you sure?" Destructive confirm button uses `state-danger`; cancel/dismiss is always the visually lower-emphasis option, never equal-weighted, so the safe path is also the visually easier path.

## 7. Navigation
Primary nav uses `surface-nav` (brand-coloured, not neutral) per Volume 14 §1 — this is deliberate since navigation is where brand identity lives most persistently on screen. Active item uses filled-icon convention from Volume 13 §3. Breadcrumbs use `text-medium`, current page in `text-high`, never truncated without a tooltip revealing the full label.

## 8. Alerts & Toasts
Inline alerts (banner style, contextual to the screen) vs. toasts (transient, corner-anchored) are used for different severities: Critical and Danger always render inline and persistent — never as an auto-dismissing toast, since a toast that disappears before being read fails the one job a critical alert has. Success and Info may use toasts. Toast auto-dismiss timing is never shorter than 5 seconds and is paused on hover/focus.

## 9. Badges & Tags
Status badges follow Volume 13 §8 shape-plus-colour exactly — never a colour-only pill. Role/domain tags (Lab, Pharmacy, Emergency) use the domain accent colours from Volume 14 §1 at reduced saturation so they read as categorisation, not urgency.

## 10. Empty, Loading, and Error States
Every list/table/panel defines all three explicitly at build time, not as an afterthought: empty state explains why it's empty and what action resolves it (never just "No data"); loading state uses skeleton shapes matching the eventual content's layout, not a generic spinner, so layout doesn't jump on load; error state names what failed and offers a retry action inline, never a dead end.

# Volume 16 — Data Visualization System

## 1. Visualization Philosophy
A chart exists to answer a specific clinical or operational question faster than a table would — if a table answers it just as fast, use the table. Every chart in ALERA must state, implicitly through its design, what decision it's meant to support.

## 2. Vitals & Trend Charts
Line charts only, never bar charts, for anything continuous over time (heart rate, blood pressure, lab trend). Reference ranges render as a shaded horizontal band behind the line, always visible, never a separate toggle — a vital sign without its normal range is Volume 4 §5's "incomplete context" rule violated visually. Out-of-range points get a `state-danger` or `state-critical` marker dot layered on the line, sized larger than in-range points so severity is visible even in a small sparkline.

## 3. Comparison Charts
Horizontal bar charts for comparing discrete categories (department load, task counts by owner). Bars ordered by value, not alphabetically, unless the user has explicitly sorted otherwise. Never more than one semantic colour per bar set unless the categories themselves carry semantic meaning (e.g. comparing Critical vs. Routine task counts, where colour maps to Volume 14's status tokens directly).

## 4. Network & Relationship Graphs
The Care Network Graph (Volume 13 §6) is the canonical relationship visualization; no second competing graph metaphor should be introduced for similar data. Force-directed layout only where relationships are genuinely non-hierarchical (referral networks); tree layout for genuinely hierarchical data (org structure, care team reporting lines) — never force one metaphor onto data shaped like the other.

## 5. Timelines
Horizontal, left-to-right, chronological, using the Volume 4 §8 event model (clinical, workflow, communication, state-change events each get a distinct marker shape, not just colour). Dense timelines collapse same-day events into a single expandable marker rather than overlapping illegibly.

## 6. Heatmaps
Reserved for genuinely two-dimensional density data (bed occupancy by ward and hour, task load by day and team) — never used decoratively to make a dashboard "feel data-rich." Colour scale is single-hue, low-to-high saturation (never a rainbow scale, which implies false precision in the boundaries between values).

## 7. AI Confidence & Prediction Visuals
Predictions always render visually distinct from confirmed data — a dashed projection line continuing from a solid historical line, per the established dashed-means-suggested convention. Confidence bands render as a widening shaded area around the projection, communicating uncertainty growing with distance, never a single crisp predicted line presented with the same visual authority as historical fact.

## 8. Anomaly Marking
Anomalies get the same marker-dot treatment as out-of-range vitals (Section 2) rather than a separate visual system — consistency across chart types is more valuable than a bespoke treatment per chart.

## 9. Accessibility in Data Viz
Every chart ships with an accessible data-table alternative (Volume 7's "no critical task on one sensory channel" applied to visualization specifically). Colour-only encoding is never the sole differentiator between series — pattern, marker shape, or direct labelling always accompanies colour.

## 10. Anti-Generic Rules
Never use 3D charts. Never use pie charts for more than four categories. Never use dual-axis charts without an explicit, visible note of which line maps to which axis. Never use a rainbow/jet colour scale. Never chart data at a level of precision the underlying source doesn't actually have.

# Volume 17 — Platform Adaptation

## 1. Cross-Platform Philosophy
ALERA is one system with three surfaces (web, mobile, tablet), not three separate products — tokens, colour, and iconography stay identical across all three; density, input model, and information hierarchy adapt.

## 2. Web (Clinician & Admin Primary Surface)
Highest information density is permitted here — multi-column layouts, side-by-side panels, hover states fully available. Keyboard navigation is a first-class citizen, not an afterthought: every action reachable by mouse must be reachable by keyboard, with visible focus order matching visual order.

## 3. Mobile (Field, Bedside, Patient Surface)
Single-column, one primary action per screen. No hover states — every interactive element must have a clear tap-equivalent for whatever a hover reveals on web (no information hidden exclusively behind hover). Bottom-anchored primary actions (thumb-reachable zone) for the most frequent actions per role. Offline-first for any workflow a nurse or field worker might perform with unreliable connectivity — actions queue locally and sync with a visible, honest sync-status indicator (Volume 13 §8's Syncing state), never a silent failure.

## 4. Tablet
Treated as an intermediate density — two-column where web would use three, single-column collapsing further only below a defined breakpoint. Tablet is the primary bedside/ward-round surface, so touch targets stay at mobile's 44×44pt minimum even though layout density is closer to web.

## 5. Connectivity Adaptation
Ghana-context constraint carried over from the engineering principles: every surface degrades gracefully on 2G/3G — images lazy-load with a low-resolution placeholder, non-critical background sync pauses under detected poor connectivity, and critical actions (medication confirmation, escalation) always show an explicit "not yet synced" state rather than optimistically appearing complete.

## 6. Notification Parity
A critical alert must reach the user through whatever channel they're actually using at that moment — push notification on mobile, persistent in-app banner on web, without requiring the user to have the "right" surface open. No surface is allowed to be the single point of failure for a critical alert.

# Volume 18 — Content & Microcopy System

## 1. Voice in Practice
Volume 3's brand voice principles applied to actual interface copy: every button label is a verb, not a noun ("Send referral," never "Referral"). Every error message states what happened and what to do next, in that order — never just "Error" or an error code alone.

## 2. Error Message Formula
`[What happened, plainly] + [what the user can do about it]`. Example pattern: "The lab result couldn't be saved because the connection dropped. It's queued and will save automatically when connection returns." Never blame the user ("You entered invalid data") — describe the mismatch instead ("This field needs a number between 1 and 500").

## 3. Empty States
Formula: `[what would normally be here] + [the action that fills it]`. Never a bare "No results" — always name what's missing and how to add it or why it's empty (no tasks assigned yet vs. no tasks match your filter are different messages, not the same generic string).

## 4. Confirmation Copy
Every irreversible-action confirmation restates the specific consequence in plain language, per Volume 15 §6 — never a generic "Are you sure?" This is a content rule as much as a component rule; the two volumes should be read together.

## 5. Notification Copy
Structured as `[who/what] + [what happened] + [what's needed, if anything]`. Critical notifications never bury the actionable part at the end of a long sentence — lead with what needs attention.

## 6. Tone by Context
Clinical contexts: direct, precise, no hedging language ("may possibly indicate" is avoided in favour of stating the actual confidence level). Patient contexts: calm, plain-language, no jargon without an inline explanation. AI-generated content: always labelled as such in its own copy, and phrased as a suggestion ("Consider reviewing...") rather than an instruction ("You must...").

## 7. Anti-Generic Copy Rules
Never use "Oops!" in an error message. Never use exclamation points in clinical or error contexts. Never use "Something went wrong" without saying what. Never use placeholder/lorem ipsum text in shipped screens. Never use the words listed as banned in Volume 6's documentation-voice rules (user-friendly, seamless, cutting-edge, etc.) inside the product's own UI copy either — the same discipline applies to what users read, not just what's written about the system.

# Volume 19 — Dark Mode & Theming

## 1. Theming Philosophy
Dark mode is not an inverted colour filter — it's a parallel token set with the same semantic meaning, built and reviewed with equal rigour, per Volume 14's dual light/dark surface and text tables.

## 2. Parity Requirements
Every component in Volume 15 must be verified in both modes before shipping — not "should mostly work," verified. Contrast ratios are re-checked in dark mode independently; a combination that passes WCAG AA in light mode does not automatically pass in dark mode.

## 3. Semantic Colour Adjustment
Semantic tokens (success, warning, danger, critical) shift toward slightly higher lightness/lower saturation in dark mode to avoid vibrating against the dark surface — the meaning stays identical, the exact hex shifts, which is why these are tokens and not hardcoded values anywhere in the codebase.

## 4. Elevation in Dark Mode
Since shadows read poorly on dark backgrounds, elevation in dark mode is communicated primarily through surface lightness steps (Volume 14 §1's dark surface ladder) with shadows playing a secondary, subtler role — not the inverse of the light-mode approach.

## 5. Imagery & Icons in Dark Mode
Icons use the same neutral token (`text-medium`) which already has a dark-mode value defined — no separate icon-specific dark palette is needed. Photography (Volume 9) gets a subtle overlay/dimming treatment so bright imagery doesn't create harsh contrast against a dark surface.

## 6. User Control
Theme follows system preference by default; manual override is available and persists per user, per device — never forced, since clinical environments vary in ambient lighting in ways ALERA can't detect on its own.

# Volume 20 — Design System Governance

## 1. Why Governance Exists
A design system decays the moment additions happen without a check against what already exists — Volume 0's "100 things ALERA will never become" is only honoured in year two if there's a process stopping drift, not just an intention.

## 2. Adding a Component
Before a new component is proposed: check whether an existing component (Volume 15) already covers the need with a new state or variant, rather than a wholly new pattern. A genuinely new component requires a one-paragraph justification (what job it does that nothing else does) before it's built.

## 3. Adding an Icon
Follows Volume 13 §16 rule 40 directly: a documented reason no existing icon can serve, checked against the full family list before drawing anything new.

## 4. Adding a Colour or Token
No new hex value enters the codebase outside Volume 14's token file — a "just this once" colour is how templated genericness re-enters a system that fought hard to avoid it.

## 5. Versioning
Each volume carries its own version number and last-reviewed date. A change to any volume is logged with what changed and why, so six months from now it's possible to answer "why is this button red" without archaeology.

## 6. Review Cadence
The full bible gets a consistency pass (not a full rewrite) every time a new major surface ships — checking new work against Volumes 0–19, not just against itself.

## 7. Ownership
Until ALERA has a design team, Manuel is the sole approver of any addition — which makes Section 2's "check first" discipline more important, not less, since there's no second reviewer to catch drift.
